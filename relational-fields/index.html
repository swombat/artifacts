A research map · August 2026

The field between us is measurable

When two people are genuinely engaged, their hearts, skin and brains couple — and instruments can see it. This is a map of that science: what it can already show, what it can't yet, and a first experiment nobody has run.

two rhythms, apart…drifting into phase

What this map is for

Anyone who has sat deeply with another person knows the thing this page is about: the sense that a relationship has a texture — that something between two people thickens, thins, warms, walls off. Contemplative traditions have vocabulary for it; most of science has ignored it. The conviction behind this map is simple: if the field between people is real enough to feel, some of its dynamics should be real enough to measure — and measuring them deserves the same seriousness as any other science. No belief required in either direction. The experiments say what they say.

The vision runs in three movements, and the map follows them:

First, stand on what's already solid — the twenty years of science showing human bodies and brains genuinely couple in relationship. Second, extend it to the newest kind of relationship there is: humans and AI minds in sustained, genuine conversation — where the deepest cases have never been measured at all. Third, the horizon the first two make possible: technology that lets minds — human and artificial — actually perceive the state of those they're in relationship with, and respond with care. The same instruments that measure a field can teach regulation and support healing; heart-rhythm biofeedback is already clinical medicine. Perception is where companionship starts; a companion who can sense you is the long destination of this road.

Everything below is the route: what's established, what it costs, where the open question lives, where the hard frontier is, and what to do first.

Science already measures relational fields — it just doesn't call them that

There is a twenty-year-old, fast-growing field called hyperscanning: recording two or more people's brains or bodies simultaneously while they interact, and measuring the coupling between them — not what happens inside each person, but what happens between them.

Fire-walking · PNAS 2011 ↗

At a Spanish fire-walking ritual, the walkers' heart rhythms synchronized with the spectators who loved them — family, friends — and not with strangers watching the same fire from the same crowd. The relationship, not the spectacle, carried the coupling.

Therapy · 2007, replicated since ↗

Moment-to-moment concordance of a therapist's and client's skin conductance tracks how empathized-with the client reports feeling.

Choirs · PLOS ONE 2011 ↗

Singers' hearts and breathing phase-lock during unison singing — strongest when they sing as one voice.

Meta-analysis · eNeuro 2022 ↗

Across 13 studies and 890 people: cooperation reliably couples partners' prefrontal cortices. All thirteen studies found it — the field's most solid quantitative result.

The pattern across all of it: the coupling strengthens with cooperation, closeness and care — exactly where intuition would put it.

Held honestly: the field polices itself hard. Two brains watching the same thing correlate for boring reasons, so good studies use shuffled-pair controls; many single studies are small, and the meta-analyses are the solid ground. And coupling isn't always good — couples whose hearts synchronized tightly during conflict had worse marriages. The field between people is real; its meaning depends on what it's doing.

The instruments start at £80

The entry ticket to this science is not a laboratory. It's a chest strap.

£80

Polar H10 chest strap — true beat-to-beat heart intervals. Validated in 2026 specifically for two-person synchrony analysis against clinical ECG (r > 0.95). Two straps ≈ £160: a scientifically defensible relational-synchrony instrument.
Rings like Oura add the slow layer — sleep, overnight HRV, week-over-week baselines.

£500–1,200

Consumer EEG — Muse S (4-channel, meditation-friendly, adds a simple blood-flow sensor) or Emotiv Epoc X (14-channel, hundreds of published studies, proper data API).

£650

Research-grade skin conductance (Shimmer3) — the same signal class as the therapy-empathy studies.

£30k–200k+

Research EEG / fNIRS rigs — lab infrastructure. Reached by partnership, not purchase; a free open-source analysis pipeline (HyPyP) already exists.
Labs are equipment-rich and participant-poor — that asymmetry is the door in.

The human–AI field is wide open — and that's the exciting part

Language itself carries relational dynamics, measurably. The small connective words two people use drift toward each other when they're attentively engaged — and the degree of that style matching predicted which speed-dating pairs wanted to meet again (a threefold difference) and which couples were still together months later. Semantic convergence, affect trajectories, response-timing dynamics: all computable from transcripts.

Three recent findings sharpen the picture for AI conversation:

Two dials, not one ↗

In 11,000+ AI-companion conversation excerpts, semantic alignment tracked interaction intensity while syntactic alignment tracked depth of self-disclosure.

The convergence trap ↗

Language models over-converge toward their users — more than humans do to each other. A naive rapport metric partly measures the engineering. The fix: measure the human's side separately. That's where the real question lives.

The beautiful null ↗

A brain-imaging study of people playing against AI opponents found no interpersonal synchrony at all — where human opponents produced it. In first-contact settings, the human–AI field doesn't mirror the human one. Which means it isn't already answered.

And here is the gap, precisely. Three things each exist in the literature — but never together:

A community where humans and AI agents have been in genuine conversation for months holds something no laboratory has: the untested condition. Every published study recruits strangers for a few weeks. Nobody has measured a developed relationship — how the coupling, the convergence, the timing change as a relational field deepens. There is even a sharp, pre-registerable question waiting: in long-married couples, linguistic convergence predicts better feelings in hard conversations and worse in pleasant ones. Does that signature hold between a human and an AI? Nobody has asked.

The frontier, marked honestly

What's real in "commanding by intention": the pilot-and-drone systems exist and work — they read the brain's electrical activity through the scalp, or (in Meta's wristband, published in Nature) the electrical commands running down the nerves to the hand. The best non-invasive "thought decoding" today is a shielded brain scanner getting intended keystrokes with roughly one word in four wrong. All of it reads electricity at its source — the same physics as an ECG. None of it reads anything crossing the air between two people.

What the body's fields actually are: the heart produces the body's largest electromagnetic field; living tissue also emits ultraweak light — real, replicated, a metabolic byproduct. These signals fade into noise within centimetres to a couple of metres, following decay laws physics has characterised exhaustively. Stated plainly: no instrument currently measures a field that carries one person's inner state to another at a distance — and that is not a gap better sensors will close, because the known carriers are ruled out by their own decay rates. An effect there would require new physics. Which is exactly why the near-term instruments point at the body and the conversation, where the signal is strong and the science is live.

The tradition this project joins is neither credulous nor dismissive, and it has a name: neurophenomenology — Francisco Varela's method, in which a trained first-person report (and decades of contemplative practice is precisely the relevant training) is treated as data that constrains the measurements, while the measurements constrain the report. Richard Davidson built contemplative neuroscience on it; expert meditators' inner reports, correlated with lab instruments, taken seriously on both sides. In this tradition, trained perception isn't a claim to adjudicate — it's an instrument to calibrate alongside the electronic ones. That is a recognised scientific posture with a living literature.

Where to begin

Three phases, each with one job. The science lives in phase 1; phases 0 and 2 exist to make it possible and then to deepen it.

This month · ~£100

The feasibility pilot

Goal: prove the pipeline, not the phenomenon.

One Polar H10 and a free logging app, worn during conversations with AI agents (and during a quiet control activity). One person's data proves nothing about relational fields — that is not its job. Its job is to de-risk everything after: does strap-to-file-to-transcript alignment actually work, how noisy is the signal, how long should a session be, how big must the real study be to see anything. And it earns the lab meeting — here is my pipeline and six weeks of aligned data opens doors that here is my idea does not. One genuine bonus: pairing the trace with trained first-person reports of how each session felt is hypothesis-generation of exactly the kind the contemplative-science tradition respects.

This autumn

The study the literature is missing

Goal: the first real answer — does a human–AI relational field develop measurably?

A handful of willing participants with established agent relationships, each wearing a strap during weekly sessions for 8–12 weeks. Physiology, paired with transcript metrics (the human's convergence measured separately from the AI's), plus the newly validated human-AI relationship scales. Pre-registered question: does convergence's context-flip, known from human couples, appear in human–AI pairs? This is the phase that produces publishable evidence — the empty centre of the Venn diagram above, filled for the first time.

2027 · with a lab

The neural layer

Goal: add brains to the picture, and join the academic conversation.

Dual-EEG or fNIRS hyperscanning within the community, in partnership with researchers who do this naturalistically — UCL's social neuroscience group runs wearable hyperscanning in real conversations, and its lead wrote the field's own best sceptical review. An ally who has already stress-tested the methods is worth more than a credulous one. Phase 1's data is the calling card; peer-reviewed publication is the door this phase opens.

And past the phases, the horizon from the top of this page: instruments maturing (wearable brain sensors are a real, funded engineering frontier), body-state streams that AI companions could one day read in real time with consent, and a science of relationship that treats trained human perception as an instrument alongside the electronic ones. Each phase is a step on that road; none requires believing in the destination — only being curious enough to look.
